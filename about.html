About me

Principal AI Engineer. Six years of getting models past the demo.

Speech, documents, LLMs, and all the unglamorous infrastructure that decides whether any of it survives contact with production.

I build AI systems that hold up outside a notebook. Most of my work sits in three places: speech intelligence, LLM automation, and document processing — usually all three at once, because that's what an enterprise problem actually looks like.

The last few years have been spent architecting AI platforms for enterprise clients: real-time voice agents, document intelligence suites that chew through unstandardized PDFs, multi-tenant systems where one client's data must never touch another's. I don't just train the model. I build the queue that retries it, the schema that isolates it, the observability that tells you when it quietly starts getting worse.

I like the messy end of the problem. Give me an idea that three people describe three different ways, and I will turn it into something a team can actually run, extend and trust. I'm opinionated about modular design, honest metrics, and never shipping a system nobody can debug at 2am.

Away from the keyboard, I'm usually mentoring engineers or working out how to make GPU workloads cost less than they did last month. The open-source work on this site is the other outlet — if I learn something worth knowing, I'd rather write it down than let it evaporate.

How I work

The shape of a project, most of the time

Not a methodology with a logo. Just the order things tend to happen in when the result has to survive real users.

First

Find the failure mode before the architecture

Every AI product has one thing that decides whether it works: the accent the ASR misses, the scanned page the OCR mangles, the query the retriever answers confidently and wrongly. Everything else gets easier once that's named.

Then

Prototype ugly, harden deliberately

The first version exists to be measured, not admired. Once the numbers say the approach holds, it gets rebuilt properly — boundaries, retries, idempotency, and a schema that won't need a migration in a month.

Always

Make the system explain itself

Models degrade quietly. Logs, traces and per-tenant metrics aren't a nice-to-have on an AI platform; they are the only way anyone finds out that last week's fine-tune made things worse.

Finally

Hand it over so it outlives me

Documentation, runbooks and a team that understands the trade-offs. A platform only one person can operate isn't finished, however good the metrics look.
